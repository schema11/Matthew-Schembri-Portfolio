Gripper classification

Industrial grippers are usually grouped by how they hold the object, which frames the design choice before any dimensions are set:

  • Impactive — jaws clamp directly onto the object. Simple, tolerant, the common choice for printed prototypes.
  • Ingressive — pins or needles penetrate the surface. Used for textiles and fibrous material.
  • Astrictive — suction, magnetic or electrostatic attraction, with no enveloping jaws.
  • Contigutive — direct contact adhesion, such as glue or freezing.

Attaching the load to the motor shaft

How torque actually crosses from shaft to mechanism determines whether the design survives: set screw onto a D-shaft or flat, keyed shaft, interference fit, clamp or split hub, or a moulded servo horn and spline. A printed hub on a smooth round shaft slips — the failure is invariably at this interface rather than in the structure.

Gearing

Spur gearing trades speed for torque at the ratio: output torque multiplies while output speed divides, minus efficiency losses. The imperfections matter more than the ideal ratio in a printed assembly — backlash from layer resolution and clearance, tooth profile error, centre-distance error from print shrinkage, and wear on plastic flanks under load.

Designing for FDM printing

  • Orient parts so load runs in-plane, never across layer boundaries.
  • Perimeters carry bending load far more efficiently than infill — four walls beats dense infill per gram.
  • Allow clearance at moving joints (typically 0.2–0.4 mm) and plan for a reaming pass.
  • Avoid unsupported overhangs beyond ~45°; design fillets at stress risers rather than sharp internal corners.
  • Use heat-set inserts or captive nuts rather than threading directly into plastic.
  • PLA creeps under sustained load and softens near 55 °C — relevant if a motor sits against the part.
